Section 1: How Diuretics Work in the Kidney

Diuretics increase renal excretion of sodium and/or water. Their effects depend on where they act in the nephron, how they reach their tubular target, and the patient’s kidney function and volume status.

1.2 Loop Diuretics & Overcoming Diuretic Resistance

How they work:

Furosemide inhibits NKCC2 in the thick ascending limb, where approximately 25% of filtered sodium is normally reabsorbed.

Delivery to the kidney:

Furosemide is highly protein-bound and reaches its luminal target through proximal tubular organic anion transporters (OAT). Reduced tubular secretion and other adaptations may contribute to diuretic resistance in kidney disease.

Sequential nephron blockade:

In selected patients, adding metolazone or IV chlorothiazide blocks downstream sodium reabsorption. Monitor urine output, blood pressure, kidney function and electrolytes closely.

1.3 Osmotic Diuretics (Mannitol)

Tubular mechanism: Filtered mannitol remains in the tubular lumen, increasing osmotic retention of water and promoting urine flow when kidney function is adequate.

Brain effect: An osmotic gradient can shift water out of brain tissue, lowering intracranial pressure in appropriate patients.

Critical warning: Anuria and pulmonary edema are important contraindications. Mannitol may transiently expand intravascular volume and worsen cardiopulmonary congestion.

Section 2: Glycemic Emergencies (DKA vs. HHS) & Diabetes Medications

Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS) are life-threatening emergencies involving insulin deficiency, dehydration and electrolyte abnormalities. DKA features clinically important ketosis and acidosis; HHS features profound hyperglycemia and hyperosmolality.

2.2 Potassium Safety & Insulin Titration

Insulin moves potassium into cells. Total-body potassium is often depleted even when the first serum potassium level appears normal or elevated. Check potassium before insulin and reassess frequently during treatment.

  • Potassium below 3.5 mmol/L: Delay insulin and begin potassium replacement with cardiac/renal monitoring until K+ exceeds 3.5 mmol/L, per the 2024 consensus report.
  • Insulin therapy: For most adult DKA, use a fixed-rate IV insulin infusion (often 0.1 units/kg/h), without a routine IV bolus. Pure HHS generally uses a lower initial rate (often 0.05 units/kg/h), after appropriate fluid and potassium assessment.
  • Electrolyte surveillance: Potassium replacement is tailored to serial laboratory values, urine output and institutional protocols; avoid automatic replacement in hyperkalemia or severe oliguria.

2.3 Non-Insulin Diabetes Drug Hazards

SGLT2 inhibitors (e.g., empagliflozin):

Can contribute to euglycemic DKA during acute illness, fasting or surgery. A near-normal glucose level does not exclude DKA.

Metformin:

Metformin-associated lactic acidosis is rare but serious, particularly with marked kidney dysfunction, hypoperfusion or other conditions that impair drug clearance.

Section 3: Abdominal Trauma Resuscitation & Massive Transfusion

Severe blunt or penetrating abdominal injury can cause rapid internal hemorrhage, shock, coagulopathy and hypothermia. Resuscitation must occur alongside rapid hemorrhage control—not replace it.

3.1 Damage-Control Resuscitation Pathway

Step 1 — Activate MTP: Use balanced blood-component resuscitation (commonly approximating RBC:plasma:platelets at 1:1:1 by component units or adult platelet dose), or whole blood where institutionally available. Avoid large volumes of crystalloid.

Step 2 — Support coagulation: Consider TXA promptly in bleeding trauma patients meeting local indications, ideally within 3 hours of injury: common regimen 1 g IV over 10 min followed by 1 g over 8 h. Follow trauma protocols.

Step 3 — Monitor and correct calcium: Citrate in transfused blood products binds ionized calcium. Measure ionized calcium repeatedly, correct hypocalcemia and monitor cardiac function. Calcium chloride requires appropriate IV access because extravasation can injure tissue.

Step 4 — Stop the bleeding: Coordinate early surgery or interventional radiology. Monitor for abdominal compartment syndrome when clinically suspected; bladder pressure provides an indirect measure of intra-abdominal pressure.

3.2 Damage Control & Citrate Toxicity

Why limit crystalloid:

Excess crystalloid can worsen dilutional coagulopathy, hypothermia and tissue edema without correcting oxygen-carrying capacity.

Why calcium matters:

Hypocalcemia impairs myocardial contraction and coagulation. Replacement should follow ionized calcium results and the local MTP protocol rather than a fixed dose after every four units in all patients.
